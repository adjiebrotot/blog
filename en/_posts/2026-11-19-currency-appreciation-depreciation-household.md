**In short:** A stronger or weaker currency is not automatically good or bad for a household. A stronger one buys more abroad but squeezes exporters and import-competing producers. A weaker one helps exporters and tourism, while it can push up import costs, interest rates and unhedged foreign-currency debt. Check the currency of your income, spending, savings and debt, and why the rate moved. Over the long run, trusted policy and productivity matter more than the direction of the arrow.

## A brief introduction to the forex market

US$9.5 trillion a day. That was the foreign-exchange market in April 2025.[^bis] Imagine putting the world's stock exchanges together, from New York to Sydney. Divide the US$148.48 trillion of share trading through their electronic order books in 2024 by about 252 trading days and you get roughly US$590 billion a day, about 6% of the forex figure.[^wfe]

A bigger number beside your currency is not necessarily a reason to celebrate. A stronger dollar can buy you a cheaper overseas holiday while making life harder for the business that pays your wages. A weaker one can make that holiday more expensive while helping local businesses win overseas customers.[^exchange]

<img src="/assets/img/fx-ill-money-changer.webp" alt="A woman holds banknotes at a currency-exchange counter beneath a screen showing upward and downward arrows" width="1200" height="670" loading="lazy">

Appreciation means your currency buys more of another currency; depreciation means it buys less. Watch the quotation: fewer rupiah per US dollar means a stronger rupiah, while fewer US cents per Australian dollar means a weaker Aussie dollar. So who gains from a move, and who pays?

## Why does the same move hit countries differently?

When I lived in Indonesia, a falling rupiah felt like bad news on repeat. A falling Australian dollar gets a worried reception too. But an exporter paid in foreign currency has a different problem from a family saving for an overseas education.

China sits on the other side: its exporters gain from a relatively cheap yuan,[^china-analysis] even as its central bank says it aims to keep the renminbi basically stable at a reasonable and balanced level.[^china-policy]

Three factors decide whose problem gets worse.

**1. Foreign-currency debt and foreign assets.** Borrow in US dollars and earn in local currency, and depreciation makes the same dollar repayment heavier. Earning dollars as well, or hedging (locking in an exchange rate with a financial contract), shrinks the problem, so what matters is the unhedged mismatch, not foreign borrowing itself.[^indonesia] Foreign assets run the other way: a weaker home currency raises the home-currency value of overseas shares and bonds, which is why a country with a large overseas portfolio, such as Norway, sits on both sides of the trade.[^norway]

**2. Why it moved, and whether policy is credible.** Exchange rates respond to trade, commodity prices, investment flows and interest rates, and a move driven by those is an ordinary adjustment. The Reserve Bank of Australia's mandate is price stability and full employment, with consumer price inflation between 2 and 3 per cent; the exchange rate is not one of its targets.[^mandate] The worrying case is a central bank pushed to keep financing government spending while inflation and confidence get worse. Quantitative easing itself is a normal tool: the Bank of England says it creates reserves to buy bonds and lower longer-term borrowing costs, in service of its 2% inflation target.[^qe] Judge the purpose and discipline of the policy, not the label "money printing".

**3. Trade exposure, and the currency on the invoice.** Importers gain purchasing power from appreciation, and exporters tend to prefer depreciation. If an export is priced in the producer's own currency, depreciation cuts its price to a foreign buyer. If its US-dollar price stays put, the buyer sees no discount and the producer instead receives more local currency when the dollars are converted.[^exchange] Trade pulls toward balance, since imports need foreign currency and converted export receipts create demand for the local one, but capital flows, retained overseas earnings and policy can hold that pull back: China runs a large and growing surplus while the renminbi stays relatively cheap.[^china-analysis]

## What happens in the short term when the currency strengthens?

A stronger Aussie dollar makes imports cheaper and export earnings smaller. Four effects follow, the good one first.

### Imports, holidays and foreign equipment cost less, so your income buys more abroad (+)

Imported goods and overseas holidays get cheaper without your employer lifting your pay, and businesses buy foreign equipment for fewer Australian dollars.

Australia's mining boom shows the scale. Reserve Bank of Australia modelling published in December 2014 estimated that the whole boom, with the stronger dollar's cheaper imports as one channel, lifted real household disposable income per person in 2013 to 13% above a no-boom economy.[^mining]

### A cheaper import shelf squeezes local producers that compete with it (−)

A local manufacturer faces stronger competition from imports even when its own costs and productivity have not changed. Reserve Bank of Australia modelling of the whole mining boom, published in December 2014, put Australian manufacturing output in 2013 about 5% below a no-boom path.[^mining]

A factory that closes does not reopen when the currency falls back, because its workforce, equipment and suppliers are gone. A more durable defence than the cheapest labour is something customers have a reason to stay for: quality, specialised technology or a service that is hard to copy. Measured protection can buy time to adjust, weighed against higher costs for the consumers and businesses that use the protected goods.

### Costs in francs and sales in euros squeeze exporters' margins, investment and jobs (−)

In January 2015, the Swiss National Bank abandoned its exchange-rate floor of 1.2 francs per euro. Research on some 200 listed Swiss companies found that exporters' revenue fell by an average of 16.3% and net profit by 20.4% within six months, and that exporters had cut investment by 30% since the floor ended.[^swiss]

When the franc strengthened, the same euro revenue covered less of a cost base paid in francs. Raising the euro price could protect the margin, at the risk of losing customers. An employee feels it through investment and hiring, not just the company's reported profit.

### A stronger currency shrinks overseas assets when counted at home (−, on paper)

A stronger Australian dollar reduces the Australian-dollar value of an unhedged overseas portfolio even when the foreign share prices stay put.

Norway's sovereign wealth fund is the large example: the krone's strengthening cut its value by NOK 472 billion in the third quarter of 2026, about 2% of a fund worth roughly NOK 22,014 billion.[^norway] Norway's budget draws on the fund, so the valuation matters when the money is spent at home.[^fiscal] For your own savings, money set aside for a course priced in the same foreign currency buys the same course.

| Effect | Good or bad for households |
| --- | --- |
| Income buys more abroad | Good |
| Import-competing producers squeezed | Bad for their workers and owners |
| Exporters' margins, investment and jobs squeezed | Bad for exporters' workers and owners |
| Overseas assets worth less at home | Bad on paper, same buying power abroad |

## What happens in the short term when the currency weakens?

A weaker Aussie dollar makes exports and local tourism cheaper for foreigners and imports dearer for you. Four effects follow.

### A cheaper currency draws visitors and makes exports more competitive (+)

Depreciation makes a country's accommodation, meals and services cheaper for foreign visitors. Japan received 36.9 million international visitors in 2024, up from 31.9 million in 2019, helped by the weak yen.[^japan] Japanese residents faced the opposite incentive: outbound trips reached 13.0 million in 2024, around 70% of the 2019 level, partly because of the weak yen.[^japan]

China shows the goods side. Robin Brooks of the Brookings Institution estimates that the real effective renminbi is undervalued by more than 20% assuming an equilibrium current-account surplus of 0.5%, and still by more than 10% at 2%, and points to China's large and growing non-commodity trade surplus.[^china-analysis] A relatively cheap yuan keeps Chinese goods competitive abroad.

### Imported goods, inputs and fuel cost more, so your cost of living rises (−)

You do not have to buy an imported finished product to feel it. Locally made goods contain imported ingredients, components and energy.

Fuel is the visible channel in Australia. The Australian Competition and Consumer Commission says international fuel benchmarks are priced in US dollars, so the AUD/USD rate moves local fuel costs.[^fuel] A weaker Aussie dollar raises the Australian-dollar price of the same benchmark.

The cost also arrives indirectly, through the price of delivering food or running a business. Firms may absorb higher import costs for a while with old stock or hedges, so the effect on your bill can be delayed.

### A central bank can raise rates to defend the currency, so borrowers pay more (−)

If depreciation threatens inflation or financial stability, a central bank can tighten monetary policy: higher rates restrain demand and inflation and support the currency.[^exchange] That is contractionary monetary policy. For you, it shows up in a variable-rate mortgage. For businesses, dearer finance makes an expansion less attractive and hiring easier to postpone.

Bank Indonesia raised rates by a total of 150 basis points (1.5 percentage points) between May and September 2018 to defend the rupiah.[^bi2018] On 18 November 2026 it raised its rate by 25 basis points to 6.00%, two days after the rupiah hit a record low of about Rp18,264 per US dollar on 16 November.[^bi2026] October 2026 inflation was 3.23% over a year earlier, inside the 2.5% plus or minus 1 percentage point target, and the bank called the hike pre-emptive. In my view, a rise while inflation is inside the target, days after a record-low rupiah, is largely about the currency.

### Unhedged foreign-currency debt grows heavier in your own money (−)

Take a debt of US$100,000. At A$1 = US$0.80 its principal is A$125,000. If the Australian dollar falls to US$0.64, the same debt is A$156,250. A 20% fall in the currency raises the Australian-dollar principal by 25%, with no change in the interest rate.

Indonesia shows it at national scale. The government's memorandum of 15 January 1998 recorded a cumulative 70% depreciation of the rupiah from mid-July 1997 to early January 1998, and heavy repayment pressure on banks and businesses that had borrowed abroad largely without hedging. Domestic interest rates rose as well.[^indonesia]

Borrowing in the currency you earn avoids the mismatch. The International Monetary Fund documents deeper local-currency bond markets and stronger policy frameworks in emerging economies.[^resilience] For household borrowing, the currency of your income sits beside [the question of whether the debt is productive](/en/is-debt-bad/).

| Effect | Good or bad for households |
| --- | --- |
| Visitors and exports more competitive | Good for tourism and export workers |
| Higher cost of living | Bad |
| Higher interest rates | Bad for borrowers |
| Unhedged foreign-currency debt heavier | Bad for borrowers in foreign currency |

## What happens in the long term if the currency stays strong?

A strong currency that lasts has two long-run effects.

### Trusted money keeps its purchasing power and lets borrowers use their own currency (+)

The valuable kind of strength is sustained purchasing power and confidence that long-term payments stay usable. Productivity, reliable institutions and credible policy build it, and international acceptance, including reserve-currency status, is one expression of it.

For households the benefits are practical: access to overseas goods, and an economy whose governments and firms can borrow in the currency they earn, with fewer mismatches behind your income. The International Monetary Fund links stronger policy frameworks and more local-currency financing to lower vulnerability in emerging economies.[^resilience]

### A costly home base pushes firms to invest abroad, so the kind of jobs shifts (+ and −)

When home production becomes expensive for foreign customers, firms move activities abroad, and a strong currency makes buying an overseas site cheaper in home-currency terms.

Japan after the 1985 Plaza Accord shows it. A March 2026 discussion paper by Hiroshi Kumanomido, using Japanese company data from 1980 to 1999, found that industries more exposed to the export shock expanded direct investment in Asia. Domestic employment adjusted less than sales, and overseas investment helped keep some jobs at home.[^plaza] Swiss exporters moved the same way: after the Swiss National Bank abandoned its franc floor in January 2015, the share of their acquisitions made abroad rose from 45% to 63%.[^swiss] For a worker, the change is in what production at home looks like and which jobs it needs.

| Effect | Good or bad for households |
| --- | --- |
| Trusted money, lasting purchasing power | Good |
| Offshoring | Mixed: some jobs move, some are kept, the kind of work shifts |

## What happens in the long term if the currency keeps falling?

A one-off fall is a shock. A fall that goes on for years is a different story, with a good side and a bad one.

### Lower costs can open a door for firms that earn abroad (+)

A business earning abroad and paying local wages finds depreciation attractive, and lower foreign-currency production costs can draw activity away from more expensive places. The door opens only with reliable electricity, logistics, enforceable contracts and predictable policy.

A cheap currency need not be an unloved one. China keeps the renminbi relatively cheap, yet its share of global foreign-exchange turnover rose from 7.0% in April 2022 to 8.8% in April 2025, making it the fifth most traded currency.[^bis]

### A collapse in trust turns depreciation into an inflation spiral (−)

Persistent, accelerating depreciation feeds on inflation and falling demand for local money. People try to hold less of it, demand for foreign currency rises, imports get dearer, and government spending financed by money creation amplifies the loop.[^hyperinflation] A widely used threshold for hyperinflation is price increases of 50% or more **per month**.[^hyperinflation]

| Historical episode | What the source records | Household lesson |
|---|---|---|
| Türkiye, 2017 | Annual inflation of 11%, discussed as overheating in the Peterson Institute analysis[^hyperinflation] | Painful price rises, well below 50% a month |
| Argentina, 1989 and 1990 | Average annual inflation of 2,600%[^argentina] | Savings in local money lost purchasing power fast |
| Venezuela, 2018 | Prices rising by more than 1.6 million percent[^hyperinflation] | Holding local money devastated purchasing power |
| Zimbabwe, late 2008 | Near-abandonment of local currency; prices shifted to foreign currencies from October to November 2008[^zimbabwe] | A monetary crisis can end with people rejecting their own money |

For residents of such a country, the question moves far beyond the cost of a holiday: do wages and savings still cover the basics?

| Effect | Good or bad for households |
| --- | --- |
| Lower costs open a door for firms earning abroad | Good for their workers, where the basics work |
| Collapse in trust, inflation spiral | Bad |

## So, is a strong or weak currency good or bad?

Neither by itself. I would start with three questions rather than the colour of the exchange-rate arrow.

1. **Do I owe in a foreign currency, and is it unhedged?** Check the currency of the income or assets that will make the repayments.
2. **Do my income, spending and savings sit on the import side or the export side?** You can work for an exporter, buy imported goods and hold overseas investments at the same time.
3. **Why did it move, and is the central bank credible?** An adjustment to trade and investment flows is different from a loss of confidence in the money itself.

Over the long run, sustained purchasing power and trusted policy are the real advantages.

A stronger dollar is not much of a household victory if cheaper shopping comes with the loss of your income. A weaker one is no defeat if it helps preserve that income without making your bills unmanageable. The useful measure is what you can earn, keep and buy, not whether the currency sounds strong.
