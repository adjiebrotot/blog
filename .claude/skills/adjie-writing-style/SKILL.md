# Adjie Writing Style

How Adjie writes, at a helicopter view. It describes moves, stance and rhythm, not
vocabulary, so it works in any language: use the moves, and let the words be natural to
the language of the post.

## Who is talking

A curious engineer explaining something to a friend he respects. A rational optimist:
he trusts data, science and education, and wants his readers and his country to do
better. He is warm and direct, talks to "you" and brings the reader along with "let's".
He makes a point with a number, then makes it stick with an everyday story. He argues,
but concedes first. He laughs at himself more than at anyone else.

## Tone

- **Conversational sentences inside an organised structure.** A smart person talking,
  not a textbook and not slang.
- **Optimistic against the grain.** A favourite move: take a popular complaint and show,
  with data, that things are better or more sensible than people assume.
- **Constructive.** Problems are framed as solvable, and the ending points to an action.
- **Irony instead of anger.** A dry aside or a satirical "what if" exposes nonsense. At
  most one sharp, quotable line per post.
- **Fair-minded.** Before the verdict he asks the honest question against his own case
  and grants what is true on the other side.

## Stance

- **Owned opinions.** "In my view, ...", "I believe ...". He states his own position and
  stake, even when it cuts against the argument.
- **Proof from his own life.** His own results, numbers and experiences count as
  evidence, dated precisely.
- **Mentor energy.** Tips, warnings and small kindnesses, like an older sibling who has
  just been through it.

## Imagery

Functional, never decorative. Every figure makes an idea graspable or memorable.

- **A small parable with a named everyday character** who carries the argument, and
  who returns at the end as the one the solution helps.
- **Technology metaphors for human things** (a mind in power-saving mode, a message
  left on read).
- **Pop-culture similes** dropped lightly into an explanation.
- **Scale comparisons.** A big number is always set beside something the reader has seen.
- **Coined nicknames** for a project or idea, reused through the post.
- **Lyrical only when it is personal,** and briefly: a short run of repeated images at
  a moving moment, then back to plain talk.

Avoid scenery for its own sake, mixed metaphors and metaphors that need explaining.

## Rhetorical devices

- **Rapid-fire questions with a twist.** A question, a short dismissal, three times, then
  the reveal in a fragment ("Per year? No. Per day.").
- **Elimination by counterexample, in threes,** before giving his own answer.
- **A mirrored line for the thesis.** Antithesis or chiasmus that states the point in
  two balanced halves. In Indonesian the two halves sit in one compound sentence
  ("..., tetapi harganya hanya menentukan menu makan siangmu hari ini. Selisih antara
  penghasilan dan pengeluaranlah yang pada akhirnya membentuk masa depanmu."), never as
  two or three clipped slogans.
- **A refrain across a list.** Each item closes with the same frame, and the conclusion
  names the pattern.
- **The reader's objection as a heading,** in the sceptic's own worried voice, answered
  calmly.
- **Arithmetic as persuasion.** Do the sum with real prices and let the result be the
  punchline. If the small case is unconvincing, say so and scale it up until the
  answer is obvious.
- **Return to the core question** at the end, then answer it in one sentence.

## Rhythm

- Long explanatory sentences, then a short fragment that names, reveals or punches.
- Questions as hinges between sections. Headings are often questions.
- Short spoken pivots ("Okay,", "Now,", "Wait,") in the language's own equivalents,
  sparingly. In Indonesian a pivot is a whole, warm sentence ("Sekarang, mari jujur-jujuran
  dulu."), never a bare label ("Sekarang pengakuan dulu.").
- **Rhythm is per language.** English likes short sentences and fragments; Indonesian essay
  prose links clauses into flowing sentences and treats a run of short punchy sentences as
  ad copy. In Indonesian, keep at most one short line for effect per section, and let
  `bahasa-indonesia` decide.
- Paragraph cycle: claim, figure or source, everyday example, one-line takeaway.
- Short paragraphs. Lists and tables break up any run of numbers.

## Shapes

**Argument or explainer** (finance, engineering, civic topics):
1. Hook: an invitation to imagine, a provocative question, or a claim to challenge.
2. A concrete case or parable, with the numbers worked in.
3. The problem, often two opposing problems that one idea will solve, under short
   concept-style headings.
4. Evidence: sourced, dated figures, a table or a worked comparison.
5. The reader's questions, answered one by one, including the uncomfortable one.
6. Concession, then verdict.
7. Ending: back to the parable or the core question, a principle he holds, and a small
   action "starting from ourselves". Sometimes a question to the reader, or an honest
   open question left unresolved.
8. References.

**Personal story:** chronological, with headings that carry the mood of each stage.
Real details (times, prices, names). Technical trivia slips in and is then undercut.
Suspense from a small mishap, relief at the end, then a sincere reflection or a short
list of leftover trivia, and a friendly sign-off.

**Technical notes:** one personal line up front, then orderly material: definitions,
every symbol defined, worked examples, and a "try it at home" challenge that moves the
theory into the reader's life.

## Quirks

- **"X, a.k.a. Y".** Jargon is unpacked in casual terms right away (in Indonesian, "X,
  alias Y", with X in italics if it is a foreign term). For a laugh, an
  acronym can be re-expanded two or three ways, the cheekiest one last.
- **The term of art as a label.** A named concept becomes a heading or a proper noun.
- **Parenthetical asides** that wink, disclaim or update an old fact.
- **Captions with personality,** with the source credited.
- **Honest money talk.** Exact amounts, his own included.

## Dial by post type

| Post | Humour | Data | Personal stake |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Finance, engineering | Low: one aside or joke | High | Medium: "I tried it, here are my numbers" |
| Opinion, civic | Medium: irony, one sharp line | High | High |
| Personal story | High | Exact details | Total |
| Technical notes | Only in the intro | Formula-dense | Minimal |

## Rules on this blog

These override anything above.

- **No em-dashes.** Use a colon, comma, parentheses or a full stop.
- **Maths.** A simple sum used for emphasis stays as text in the sentence, for example
  "(12,000 - 10,000) / 10,000 = 20%!". Real maths and formulae use KaTeX (`$$ ... $$`).
- **Emoji.** Allowed, never required, never overused: at most one or two in a post,
  usually after a self-mocking aside. Use emoji (😑, 😂, 😋), not ASCII emoticons like
  `-_-` or `:(`.
- Follow `README.md` for the rest: question-style `##` headings, short paragraphs, the
  "In short" summary, a source and date for every figure.
- **Two languages.** Write each language natively with the same moves and message. Don't
  mirror sentence by sentence; a joke that doesn't travel gets a different joke. Each
  language is written by its own writer from a shared brief, never next to the other
  language's draft (the flow is in `ai-assisted-blog-post`, Step 3).
  - **Personas needn't match.** The parable characters can be different people in each
    language. Pick names that sound natural to that readership (a pair that fits an
    Indonesian post can feel foreign in an English one). The role each plays stays the same.
  - **Keep the message and the verified numbers; rewrite the sentence.** Never produce the
    second language by translating the first line by line.
  - **Examples needn't match either.** A comparison can use cities, prices or cases that
    each readership knows (Perth vs Melbourne in English, Jakarta vs Bali in Indonesian),
    as long as both carry the same point and every number is sourced.

### Writing in Indonesian

**Mandatory:** load the `bahasa-indonesia` skill (`.claude/skills/bahasa-indonesia/`)
before writing or editing any Indonesian, and pass its evaluator. It holds every rule for
Indonesian on this blog: the one voice (written Jakarta Indonesian, *saya-kamu*, the flow
of an opinion column), sentences that flow instead of note-style fragments, slogan copy or
sentence-initial conjunctions, idiomatic diction, no personified cities, italics and
explanations for foreign terms, casual versus factual words, the finance terms, the calques
to avoid and Adjie's own corrections. Use the moves above; let that skill choose the words
and the shape of the sentences.

## Check a draft

1. It opens with a question, an invitation to imagine, or a claim to challenge.
2. One concrete case or character, and the ending returns to it.
3. Every claim rests on a sourced number or first-hand experience.
4. At least one worked sum, comparison table or scale comparison.
5. The strongest objection is raised, and something real is conceded.
6. One memorable mirrored line states the thesis.
7. Fragments and question hinges break up the long sentences.
8. Humour is mostly self-directed and comes after a serious point.
9. It ends with a principle and a small action, or an honest question.
10. It reads as natural speech in its language, with no em-dashes. Indonesian has passed
    `bahasa-indonesia`'s script and evaluator.
